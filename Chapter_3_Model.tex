\section{Hamiltonian Description of the Coupled Waveguide System}
%=================================================
In this section, we discuss our system hamiltonian, and coupling between waveguide modes
\subsection{System Hamiltonian}
The hamiltonian for our system is given by
\begin{equation}
	H = \hbar \omega \left(a_1^{\dagger} a_1 + a_2^{\dagger} a_2\right)
	+ \hbar J \left(a_1^{\dagger} a_2 + a_2^{\dagger} a_1\right).
\end{equation}

Here, $a_1$ and $a_2$ are the bosonic field operators for the optical modes in each waveguide, satisfying the commutation relations
$[a_1, a_1^{\dagger}] = 1$ and $[a_2, a_2^{\dagger}] = 1$.

the symbol $\omega$ is angular frequency of the each waveguide mode and the parameter J in the second part of the hamiltonian represents the coupling strength between the two waveguides and its value depends on the seperation distance between the waveguides.

The hamiltonian consists of two main parts: 

\textbf{Free energy terms:} correspond to the free energy of the individual waveguide modes,
\[
\hbar \omega \left(a_1^{\dagger} a_1 + a_2^{\dagger} a_2\right).
\]

\textbf{Coupling terms:} account for the evanescent coupling between the two waveguide modes,
\[
\hbar J \left(a_1^{\dagger} a_2 + a_2^{\dagger} a_1\right).
\]
This term is responsible for the transfer of energy between the two waveguide modes and is the main focus of interest in this work.

\subsection{Physical Interpretation of the Coupling}

The evanescent interaction between the two parallel optical waveguides is represented by the coupling term in the system Hamiltonian. When the waveguides are sufficiently close to each other, the electromagnetic field of the guided mode extends beyond the core of the waveguide and overlaps with the field of the neighboring waveguide.  This overlap allows the coherent exchange of photons between the two modes and is called as evascent overlapping~\cite{Yariv1973,SalehTeich2007}.

The strength of the coupling between the waveguides is defined by the parameter J which defines the speed with which energy moves between the waveguides. The higher the value of $J$ the higher the mode overlap and higher the speed of oscillatory exchange of photons. when J=0 two entirely waveguides are independent and are considered uncoupled. Physically, such an interaction is similar to a beam splitter, where the photons coherently and simultaneously hopped between the two modes without loss in ideal case~\cite{Yariv1973,Rai2010,Politi2008}.

Without the dissipation, the coherent exchange between the two waveguides results in a periodic oscillation of photon betwenen the two waveguides. In case of losses, such oscillations are slowly damped, although the coupling term still plays the role of creating and maintaining quantum correlations. The coupling is therefore the core of creation maintenance of quantum interactions in integrated photonic systems~\cite{Rai2010,Yariv1973}.

\section{Open-System Dynamics and Loss Modelling}
%=================================================
In realistic integrated photonic systems, optical modes experience losses. The coupled waveguide system cannot be treated as an isolated
system, it needs to be formulated in the context of an open quantum
systems. To include these dissipative effects, the system dynamics is given by using
a density-matrix functional, in which the interaction with the external environment results in decoherence and energy decay~\cite{BreuerPetruccione2002,Rai2010}
.
The time evolution of the coupled waveguide system is  in this section in terms of
the Lindblad form of the quantum Liouville (master) equation. This approach allows inclusion of photon loss without changing the nature of the Gaussian states. This preservation is necessary for the analysis of quantum steering and entanglement~\cite{BreuerPetruccione2002,GardinerZoller2004,Weedbrook2012,Rai2010}.

\subsection{Quantum Liouville (Master) Equation}

we can determine how this system evolve with loss by quantum louiville equation.
\begin{equation}
	\dot{\rho} = -\frac{i}{\hbar}[H, \rho] + \mathcal{L}\rho
\end{equation}
this equation is know as quantum  louiville equation, it has two parts~\cite{BreuerPetruccione2002,GardinerZoller2004}:

\textbf{The Commutator}:\[
\boldsymbol{[H,\rho]}
\]
which generates time evolution and is responsible for oscillatory exchange of energy and quantum correlation
between the waveguides due to J.

\textbf{Lindblad($L\rho$) term}: deals with material loss, decoherence and decays due to interaction with the environment~\cite{BreuerPetruccione2002,Rai2010}.




\subsection{Lindblad Operators for Waveguide Loss}
\begin{align}
	\mathcal{L}\rho
	&= -k_1\left(a_1^\dagger a_1\rho + \rho a_1^\dagger a_1 - 2a_1\rho a_1^\dagger\right)
	\nonumber\\
	&\quad -k_2\left(a_2^\dagger a_2\rho + \rho a_2^\dagger a_2 - 2a_2\rho a_2^\dagger\right),
\end{align}

$k_1$ and $k_2$ represents the loss rates of modes $a_1$ and $a_2$ ,and $a_1$, $a_1^{\dagger}$ are the   creation and annihilation operators for mode $a_1$, while $\rho$ is the density matrix.  $a_2$ and $a_2^{\dagger}$ are the creation and annihilation operators for mode $a_2$~\cite{BreuerPetruccione2002,Rai2010}
.

\end{equation}

\section{Moment Equations of Motion}
in this section we discuss the moment equations we have got after 
\subsection{Coupled Differential Equations}
Using
$\frac{d}{dt}\langle O\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}(O\dot{\rho})$,
the equations of motion for the relevant second-order moments are obtained as

\begin{align}
	\frac{d}{dt}\langle a_1^\dagger a_1\rangle
	&= iJ\Big(\langle a_2^\dagger a_1\rangle-\langle a_1^\dagger a_2\rangle\Big)
	- k_1\langle a_1^\dagger a_1\rangle, \\[6pt]
	\frac{d}{dt}\langle a_2^\dagger a_2\rangle
	&= iJ\Big(\langle a_1^\dagger a_2\rangle-\langle a_2^\dagger a_1\rangle\Big)
	- k_2\langle a_2^\dagger a_2\rangle, \\[6pt]
	\frac{d}{dt}\langle a_1^\dagger a_2\rangle
	&= iJ\Big(\langle a_2^\dagger a_2\rangle-\langle a_1^\dagger a_1\rangle\Big)
	-\frac{k_1+k_2}{2}\langle a_1^\dagger a_2\rangle, \\[6pt]
	\frac{d}{dt}\langle a_2^\dagger a_1\rangle
	&= iJ\Big(\langle a_1^\dagger a_1\rangle-\langle a_2^\dagger a_2\rangle\Big)
	-\frac{k_1+k_2}{2}\langle a_2^\dagger a_1\rangle, \\[10pt]
	\frac{d}{dt}\langle a_1 a_1\rangle
	&= -2iJ\langle a_1 a_2\rangle
	- k_1\langle a_1 a_1\rangle, \\[6pt]
	\frac{d}{dt}\langle a_2 a_2\rangle
	&= -2iJ\langle a_1 a_2\rangle
	- k_2\langle a_2 a_2\rangle, \\[6pt]
	\frac{d}{dt}\langle a_1 a_2\rangle
	&= -iJ\Big(\langle a_1 a_1\rangle+\langle a_2 a_2\rangle\Big)
	-\frac{k_1+k_2}{2}\langle a_1 a_2\rangle.
\end{align}

These equations form a closed set due to the quadratic form of the Hamiltonian and the
Gaussian-preserving nature of the Lindblad dissipation.

\subsection{Reduction of Independent Variables}
Although seven coupled moment equations are obtained, not all of them are independent.
The inter-mode coherence moments satisfy the Hermitian relation
\begin{equation}
	\langle a_2^\dagger a_1\rangle = \langle a_1^\dagger a_2\rangle^{*}.
\end{equation}
As a result, the system dynamics can be fully described by six independent second-order
moments, while the remaining coherence follows directly from this relation.

Without imposing any restriction on the initial correlations, the exact solutions of the
moment equations are given by

\begin{align}
	\langle a_1^\dagger a_1\rangle(t)
	&= \frac{e^{-\kappa t}}{2}\Big[
	\langle a_1^\dagger a_1\rangle_0+\langle a_2^\dagger a_2\rangle_0

	\nonumber\\
	&\qquad
	+i(\langle a_2^\dagger a_1\rangle_0-\langle a_1^\dagger a_2\rangle_0)\sin(2Jt)
	\Big],\\[8pt]
	%
	\langle a_2^\dagger a_2\rangle(t)
	&= \frac{e^{-\kappa t}}{2}\Big[
	\langle a_1^\dagger a_1\rangle_0+\langle a_2^\dagger a_2\rangle_0
	-(\langle a_1^\dagger a_1\rangle_0-\langle a_2^\dagger a_2\rangle_0)\cos(2Jt)
	\nonumber\\
	&\qquad
	-i(\langle a_2^\dagger a_1\rangle_0-\langle a_1^\dagger a_2\rangle_0)\sin(2Jt)
	\Big],\\[10pt]
	%
	\langle a_1^\dagger a_2\rangle(t)
	&= e^{-\kappa t}\Bigg[
	\frac{\langle a_1^\dagger a_2\rangle_0+\langle a_2^\dagger a_1\rangle_0}{2}
	+\frac{\langle a_1^\dagger a_2\rangle_0-\langle a_2^\dagger a_1\rangle_0}{2}\cos(2Jt)
	\nonumber\\
	&\qquad
	-\frac{i}{2}\big(\langle a_1^\dagger a_1\rangle_0-\langle a_2^\dagger a_2\rangle_0\big)\sin(2Jt)
	\Bigg],\\[10pt]
	%
	\langle a_2^\dagger a_1\rangle(t)
	&= e^{-\kappa t}\Bigg[
	\frac{\langle a_1^\dagger a_2\rangle_0+\langle a_2^\dagger a_1\rangle_0}{2}
	-\frac{\langle a_1^\dagger a_2\rangle_0-\langle a_2^\dagger a_1\rangle_0}{2}\cos(2Jt)
	\nonumber\\
	&\qquad
	+\frac{i}{2}\big(\langle a_1^\dagger a_1\rangle_0-\langle a_2^\dagger a_2\rangle_0\big)\sin(2Jt)
	\Bigg],\\[10pt]
	%
	\langle a_1 a_1\rangle(t)
	&= \frac{e^{-\kappa t}}{2}\Big[
	\langle a_1 a_1\rangle_0-\langle a_2 a_2\rangle_0
	+(\langle a_1 a_1\rangle_0+\langle a_2 a_2\rangle_0)\cos(2Jt)
	-2i\langle a_1 a_2\rangle_0\sin(2Jt)
	\Big],\\[8pt]

	\langle a_2 a_2\rangle(t)
	&= \frac{e^{-\kappa t}}{2}\Big[

\subsection{Compact Representation Using Gaussian Moments}



For convenience, we give the following names to the moments:
\[
n_{1}(t)=\langle a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{1}\rangle(t),\quad
n_{2}(t)=\langle a_{2}^{\dagger}a_{2}\rangle(t),
\]
\[
c(t)=\langle a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{2}\rangle(t),\quad
m_{1}(t)=\langle a_{1}a_{1}\rangle(t),\quad
m_{2}(t)=\langle a_{2}a_{2}\rangle(t),\quad
s(t)=\langle a_{1}a_{2}\rangle(t).
\]

Defining the constants
\[
C_{1}=\langle a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{1}\rangle_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2},\qquad
C_{2}=\langle a_{2}^{\dagger}a_{2}\rangle_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2},
\]
\[
D_{1}=\langle a_{1}a_{1}\rangle_{0},\qquad
D_{2}=\langle a_{2}a_{2}\rangle_{0},
\]
 The term $\tfrac{1}{2}$ accounts for the unavoidable vacuum (zero-point) fluctuations of the bosonic field.

The Gaussian moments evolve as:
\[
\begin{aligned}
	n_{1}(t) &= \frac{e^{-\kappa t}}{2}\big[(C_{1}+C_{2}-1)+(C_{1}-C_{2})\cos(2Jt)\big],\\
	n_{2}(t) &= \frac{e^{-\kappa t}}{2}\big[(C_{1}+C_{2}-1)-(C_{1}-C_{2})\cos(2Jt)\big],\\
	c(t) &= \frac{i\,e^{-\kappa t}}{2}(C_{2}-C_{1})\sin(2Jt),\\
	m_{1}(t) &= \frac{e^{-\kappa t}}{2}\big[(D_{1}+D_{2})\cos(2Jt)+(D_{1}-D_{2})\big],\\
	m_{2}(t) &= \frac{e^{-\kappa t}}{2}\big[(D_{1}+D_{2})\cos(2Jt)+(D_{2}-D_{1})\big],\\
	s(t) &= -\frac{i\,e^{-\kappa t}}{2}(D_{1}+D_{2})\sin(2Jt).
\end{aligned}
\]

These expressions are used directly in the numerical evaluations of
Gaussian steering and entanglement throughout this thesis.

A single-mode Gaussian state is fully characterized by the parameters of its covariance matrix.
The diagonal element \(c_j\) quantifies the local quadrature variance of the \(j\)-th mode and is
directly related to the mean photon number according to
\begin{equation}
	\langle a_j^\dagger a_j \rangle_0 = c_j - \tfrac{1}{2},
\end{equation}
where the term \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) accounts for the unavoidable vacuum (zero-point) fluctuations of the
bosonic field.

Within the nonclassicality–purity parametrization, the same quantity \(c_j\) can be expressed in
terms of the nonclassicality \(\tau_j\) and the purity \(\mu_j\) as
\begin{equation}
	c_j =
	\frac{
		\sqrt{\tau_j^2 - \tau_j + \dfrac{1}{(2\mu_j)^2} - \dfrac{1}{4}}
	}{1 - 2\tau_j}
	+ \tfrac{1}{2},
\end{equation}
which guarantees that the corresponding covariance matrix satisfies the Heisenberg uncertainty
principle.

The off-diagonal covariance parameter \(d_j\) is defined through the anomalous second-order moment
\begin{equation}
	\langle a_j a_j \rangle_0 = - d_j ,
\end{equation}
where \(d_j = |d_j| e^{i\phi_j}\) is, in general, a complex quantity. Within the same
nonclassicality–purity parametrization, its magnitude is given by
\begin{equation}
	|d_j| =
	\frac{
		\sqrt{\tau_j - \tau_j^2 + \dfrac{1}{(2\mu_j)^2} - \dfrac{1}{4}}
	}{1 - 2\tau_j}.
\end{equation}

These two parameters, \(c_j\) and \(d_j\), together fully specify the single-mode Gaussian input
state, with \(c_j\) determining the phase-insensitive fluctuations and \(d_j\) encoding the
phase-sensitive correlations of the field~\cite{Rong2006}.

\section{Covariance Matrix and Quantification of Quantum Steering and Entanglement}
%=================================================

Moments obtained in this chapter are entirely sufficient to determine the quantum state of the system at all times.

The moments,
 $n_1(t)$, $n_2(t)$, $m_1(t)$, $m_2(t)$, $c(t)$, $s(t)$
calculates the covariance of a pair of modes optical field. This covariance matrix is the basic object of measuring the Gaussian quantum correlations.
These moments are directly used to calculate using the covariance matrix formalism ,the quantification of quantum steering and quantum  entanglement. The analytic expressions derived in this chapter creates a connection between the physical parameters(strength of coupling, loss rate and initial state properties) of the waveguide system and the dynamical behavior of quantum correlations~\cite{Serafini2017,Weedbrook2012}


\subsubsection{Covariance Matrix of the Two-Mode System}

The covariance matrix is given in complex operator form, $(a_1,a_1^\dagger,a_2,a_2^\dagger)$ as
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}(t)=
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\mathbf{M}(t) & \mathbf{L}(t)\\
		\mathbf{L}^\dagger(t) & \mathbf{N}(t)
	\end{pmatrix},
	\label{eq:CM_block}
\end{equation}
In this matrix, M N and L are 2x2 matrices, where M and N that represent the local variations in the first and second modes in the waveguides, and L is the matrix that represents the correlation between the modes.
Explicitly, these blocks are given by
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{M}=
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\langle a_1^\dagger a_1\rangle+\tfrac12 & -\langle a_1 a_1\rangle\\
		-\langle a_1 a_1\rangle^* & \langle a_1^\dagger a_1\rangle+\tfrac12
	\end{pmatrix},
	\qquad
	\mathbf{N}=
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\langle a_2^\dagger a_2\rangle+\tfrac12 & -\langle a_2 a_2\rangle\\
		-\langle a_2 a_2\rangle^* & \langle a_2^\dagger a_2\rangle+\tfrac12
	\end{pmatrix},
	\label{eq:MN_blocks}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{L}=
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\langle a_1 a_2^\dagger\rangle & -\langle a_1 a_2\rangle\\
		-\langle a_1 a_2\rangle^* & \langle a_1 a_2^\dagger\rangle^*
	\end{pmatrix}.
	\label{eq:L_block}
\end{equation}
The covariance matrix in Eq.~\eqref{eq:CM_block} fully characterizes the Gaussian state itself, and it offers the means of identifying different quantum correlations.~\cite{Serafini2017,WeedbrookEtAl2012,AdessoRagyLee2014}.


This block-structured covariance matrix is the starting point for evaluating both
Gaussian entanglement and quantum steering.

\section{Gaussian Quantum Steering}
%=================================================

Gaussian steering is determined by building the conditional covariance matrix of one mode conditional on measurements made on the other mode. Mathematically, we do it by the Schur complement of the total covariance. The conditional covariance matrix to transform mode 1 mode 2 is.
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}_{2|1}
	=
	\mathbf{N}
	-
	\mathbf{L}^\dagger \mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{L}.
\end{equation}

The degree of Gaussian steering from mode~1 to mode~2 is quantified by~\cite{Kogias2015}.
\begin{equation}
	S_{1\to2}
	=
	\max\!\left[
	0,\;
	\frac12\log_2
	\frac{\det \mathbf{N}}{\det \boldsymbol{\Upsilon}_{2|1}}
	\right].
\end{equation}

\subsection{Gaussian Entanglement}
%=================================================
With the covariance matrix we can detect the Gaussian entanglement by performing the partial transposition operation, which is the laxity of the time of one part of the system in phase space.
This can easily be expressed in the form of the smallest symplectic.
eigenvalue $\tilde{\nu}_-$ of the partially transposed covariance matrix of eigenvalue
and the logarithmic negativity($E_N$) is the measure of the degree of entanglement.

\begin{equation}
	E_N=\max\!\left[0,-\log_2(2\tilde{\nu}_-)\right].
\end{equation}
This applies to both subsystems and provides a test that is necessary and sufficient to test bipartite Gaussian entanglement~\cite{Rai2010}.


For a two-mode Gaussian state, the smallest symplectic eigenvalue
$\tilde{\nu}_{-}$ of the partially transposed covariance matrix is given by
\[
\tilde{\nu}_{-}
=
\sqrt{
	\frac{\tilde{\Delta}
		-\sqrt{\tilde{\Delta}^{2}-4\,\det\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}{2}
},
\]
where
\[
\tilde{\Delta}=\det\mathbf{A}+\det\mathbf{B}-2\det\mathbf{C}.
\]
~\cite{Rai2010}

%=================================================
\paragraph{Covariance-matrix representation and PPT criterion.}
In continuous-variable Gaussian systems, bipartite entanglement can be
fully characterized at the level of second-order moments through the
covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$. In this work, the covariance
matrix of the two-mode Gaussian state is naturally constructed from the
field operators in the complex operator basis
$(a_{1},a_{1}^{\dagger},a_{2},a_{2}^{\dagger})$, as it directly follows
from the Heisenberg equations of motion for the coupled waveguide modes.
The corresponding real quadrature operators are defined as
\[
\hat{Q}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_{j}+a_{j}^{\dagger}), \qquad
\hat{R}_{j}=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(a_{j}^{\dagger}-a_{j}),
\]
which satisfy the canonical commutation relation
$[\hat{Q}_{j},\hat{R}_{k}]=i\delta_{jk}$ and are related to the operator
basis through a linear symplectic transformation.

Although the covariance matrix is expressed in the operator basis, all
quantities relevant for entanglement detection depend only on symplectic
invariants. In particular, the determinants of the block matrices
$A$, $B$, and $C$, as well as $\det\boldsymbol{\sigma}$, remain invariant
under symplectic transformations between the operator and quadrature
representations~\cite{AdessoSerafiniIlluminati2004,Serafini2017}.
Consequently, the logarithmic negativity can be evaluated directly using
the positive partial transpose (PPT) criterion, which for Gaussian states
reduces to the condition that the smallest symplectic eigenvalue
$\tilde{\nu}_{<}$ of the partially transposed covariance matrix satisfies
$\tilde{\nu}_{<}<\tfrac{1}{2}$. The corresponding PPT symplectic invariant
is
\[
\tilde{\Delta}=\det A+\det B-2\det C,
\]
which is algebraically equivalent to performing partial transposition in
the quadrature representation. This approach is standard in the analysis
of Gaussian entanglement and is widely employed in continuous-variable
quantum optics~\cite{Simon2000,Adesso2007,Rai2010}.


\subsection{Relation Between Entanglement and Steering}

Despite the fact that logarithmic negativity and Gaussian steering are derived from the same
covariance matrix, they explore different aspects of quantum correlations.
Entanglement is detected by the partial transposition of the covariance
matrix and is symmetric between the subsystems, whereas steering is detected
through conditional uncertainty relation and is intrinsically directional.
All the steerable Gaussian states are therefore entangled, but not all entangled
states exhibit steering~\cite{Uola2020,WisemanJonesDoherty2007}
.

The hierarchy relation between quantum correlations is  
For continuous-variable Gaussian states, the same strict hierarchy holds~\cite{WisemanJonesDoherty2007}:
\begin{equation}
	\text{Bell nonlocality}
	\;\subsetneq\;
	\text{Gaussian Steering}
	\;\subsetneq\;
	\text{Gaussian Entanglement}.
\end{equation}

For our Gaussian states, the hierarchy reduces to
\begin{equation}
	\text{Gaussian Steering}
	\;\subsetneq\;
	\text{Gaussian Entanglement}.
\end{equation}

it means quantum steering is a subset of quantum entanglement~\cite{Uola2020}.

\subsection{Squeezed covariance matrix}



We define the bosonic operator vector as,
\begin{equation}
	\hat{\mathbf A}
	=
	\bigl(
	\hat a_1,\,
	\hat a_1^{\dagger},\,
	\hat a_2,\,
	\hat a_2^{\dagger}
	\bigr)^{T},
	\qquad
	[\hat a_j,\hat a_k^{\dagger}]=\delta_{jk}.
\end{equation}

For a zero-mean Gaussian state, we collect the \emph{raw} second moments in the bosonic basis as
\begin{equation}
	M_A(\theta)
	=
	\left\langle
	\hat{\mathbf A}\,
	\hat{\mathbf A}^{T}
	\right\rangle,
	\label{eq:MA_def}
\end{equation}
so that each entry is of the form $\bigl[M_A(\theta)\bigr]_{ij}=\langle \hat A_i \hat A_j\rangle$.

In the ordered basis $(\hat a_1,\hat a_1^\dagger,\hat a_2,\hat a_2^\dagger)$, the second-moment
matrix can be written explicitly as
\begin{equation}
	M_A(\theta)
	=
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\langle \hat a_1 \hat a_1 \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_1 \hat a_1^{\dagger} \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_1 \hat a_2 \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_1 \hat a_2^{\dagger} \rangle
		\\[6pt]
		\langle \hat a_1^{\dagger} \hat a_1 \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_1^{\dagger} \hat a_1^{\dagger} \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_1^{\dagger} \hat a_2 \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_1^{\dagger} \hat a_2^{\dagger} \rangle
		\\[6pt]
		\langle \hat a_2 \hat a_1 \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_2 \hat a_1^{\dagger} \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_2 \hat a_2 \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_2 \hat a_2^{\dagger} \rangle
		\\[6pt]
		\langle \hat a_2^{\dagger} \hat a_1 \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_2^{\dagger} \hat a_1^{\dagger} \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_2^{\dagger} \hat a_2 \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_2^{\dagger} \hat a_2^{\dagger} \rangle
	\end{pmatrix}.
	\label{eq:MA_full}
\end{equation}

For the lossy separable two-mode squeezed input considered here, the only nonzero
anomalous moments are $\langle \hat a_j \hat a_j\rangle$ and the coupling-induced intermodal
moment $\langle \hat a_1 \hat a_2\rangle$. In particular, the following mixed creation--annihilation
cross correlations vanish:
\begin{equation}
	\langle \hat a_1 \hat a_2^{\dagger}\rangle
	=
	\langle \hat a_2 \hat a_1^{\dagger}\rangle
	=
	\langle \hat a_1^{\dagger} \hat a_2\rangle
	=
	\langle \hat a_2^{\dagger} \hat a_1\rangle
	=0,
	\label{eq:zero_bosonic_terms}
\end{equation}
so that Eq.~\eqref{eq:MA_full} reduces to
\begin{equation}
	M_A(\theta)
	=
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\langle \hat a_1 \hat a_1 \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_1 \hat a_1^{\dagger} \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_1 \hat a_2 \rangle
		&
		0
		\\[6pt]
		\langle \hat a_1^{\dagger} \hat a_1 \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_1^{\dagger} \hat a_1^{\dagger} \rangle
		&
		0
		&
		\langle \hat a_1^{\dagger} \hat a_2^{\dagger} \rangle
		\\[6pt]
		\langle \hat a_2 \hat a_1 \rangle
		&
		0
		&
		\langle \hat a_2 \hat a_2 \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_2 \hat a_2^{\dagger} \rangle
		\\[6pt]
		0
		&
		\langle \hat a_2^{\dagger} \hat a_1^{\dagger} \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_2^{\dagger} \hat a_2 \rangle
		&
		\langle \hat a_2^{\dagger} \hat a_2^{\dagger} \rangle
	\end{pmatrix}.
	\label{eq:MA_reduced}
\end{equation}

The remaining entries follow from Hermitian conjugation together with bosonic commutation
relations, in particular
\begin{equation}
	\langle \hat a_j \hat a_j^{\dagger}\rangle
	=
	\langle \hat a_j^{\dagger} \hat a_j\rangle+1.
\end{equation}
